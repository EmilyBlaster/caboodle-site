first edit.

A take-home for a Lead Learning Experience Designer role at the Wikimedia Foundation. The question: how might learning help more people go from interested to a first edit, and come back for a second? Six pages, one prototype, about two hours of thinking and a few more of building.

field exercise · 01

The problem, in my words

Hundreds of millions of people read Wikipedia. Only a small share ever click Edit. Of those who start typing, about half never publish. Of those who publish, more than a quarter have their edit undone within two days. Of those whose edit survives, most never come back. The brief asked what, if anything, a Community Development team should do about it, with four to six weeks, no new product features, and a note that the answer should not be a course.

field exercise · 02

What I did first: nothing

Action Mapping starts with the outcome, not the content. The outcome here was simple to state: more first edits still standing after 48 hours, and more people back for a second edit within two weeks. Then the question is what people would need to do differently, and whether the reason they are not doing it is something they do not know. Most of the time it is not.

The analytics came as a snapshot, each stage measured against the original readers. That hides where the losses are. I re-measured each step against the step before it. Three leaks stood out, and each had a different cause. Confusion inside the editor is an interface problem, and product owns it. A reverted edit with no explanation is a consequence with no information attached. That one, learning can fix.

field exercise · 03

Three directions, one bet

The product team had asked whether people should learn before they edit or while they edit. I added a third: learn from what happens after. A short primer delivered through partner communities. Content behind the help panel the editor already has. Or two small pieces at the moments that matter most: a three-item check before Publish, and a message after a good-faith revert that says why, shows the fix in about a minute, and points to a person.

I chose the third. Every piece of evidence pointed at the revert. It serves the experienced editors' worry about quality and the product team's goal of activation at the same time. It ships with content and community partners only. And it can be tested in six weeks.

field exercise · 04

The experiment

Week one is diagnosis before anything gets built: revert data by reason, a handful of observed first edits, conversations with the volunteers who patrol new edits, and measures agreed with an analyst. Then a randomized cohort test. The guardrail is that patroller time per newcomer edit cannot go up. Every number is reported by segment, because the brief cared about women and younger people and the data did not say a word about either. The decision rules were written before the test: if nothing moves and the sessions point at the interface, hand product the notes and stop.

field exercise · 05

The artifact

A design system built from Wikimedia's published Codex tokens, so the screens would be judged as content rather than as a mockup. Two screens in the document, and a clickable walkthrough so you can step through the moments as a newcomer would. The revert screen is the whole idea in one picture.

field exercise · 06

What changed in my thinking

I came in thinking about a course. The evidence kept dragging me to the moments after the edit. The prior course had a 4.9 rating and around sixty finishers, and could not tell anyone whether it changed editing. That was the number I had to stop looking at.

field exercise · 07

How AI was used

Claude Code ran the whole way through. It converted the funnel, caught a mismatch between how the old course and the funnel measured returns, argued the experienced editor's side against my own diagnosis, pulled the Codex tokens, and built the walkthrough. The direction, the diagnosis, the copy and the decision rules are mine. Field tool, not a shortcut.
